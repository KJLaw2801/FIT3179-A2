Australia's Carbon Monoxide Emissions 23/24

State-by-State Industrial Pollution Analysis

About Carbon Monoxide Emissions:

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colorless, odorless gas that poses significant risks to both human health and environmental quality. When released into the atmosphere, CO contributes to air pollution and can form ground-level ozone, which damages ecosystems and agricultural crops. For humans, exposure to carbon monoxide can cause headaches, dizziness, and in severe cases, can be fatal by preventing oxygen from reaching vital organs.

Industrial facilities are major sources of CO emissions in Australia, making it crucial to monitor and track these emissions by state and facility. This data helps policymakers identify pollution hotspots, assess the effectiveness of environmental regulations, and protect communities from harmful exposure. By understanding patterns in emissions per capita and per facility, we can better target reduction efforts and ensure a healthier environment for all Australians.

Emission Rankings

Totals and per‑capita tell different stories: New South Wales leads in total emissions yet ranks third‑lowest per capita (225 kg per person) as its large population dilutes the load, while Tasmania is 6th by total but 1st per capita (777 kg per person), showing how smaller populations can make emissions feel more intense per resident.

The spread in per‑capita emissions is wide, ranging from around 7.4 kg per person in the Australian Capital Territory to roughly 777 kg per person in Tasmania, about a hundred-fold difference. Victoria illustrates how scale and intensity can diverge: it is mid‑pack on total emissions yet remains very low per capita (about 84.5 kg per person). Queensland, while 2nd in total emissions, sits higher per capita (about 266 kg per person) than New South Wales, showing how industrial mix and population size combine to shape intensity.

Focusing on per-capita emissions helps compare how intensely people in each state experience the pollution load. It makes comparisons fair by adjusting for population size and shows where the average resident bears a higher share. Use it alongside total emissions which shows the overall scale to address, while per-capita shows exposure and fairness, helping prioritize actions that benefit people most.

State Share Comparison

Total emissions share: Most of the national CO load comes from four states: New South Wales (28%), Queensland (22%), South Australia (16%), and Western Australia (16%). Top four combined: 83.2%. Victoria contributes a smaller slice (9%), while Tasmania (7%), the Northern Territory (1%), and the Australian Capital Territory (0.1%) make up the remainder. Use this view to spot absolute hotspots that drive the national total.

Population share: The population is concentrated in New South Wales (31%), Victoria (26%), and Queensland (21%), with Western Australia (11%) and South Australia (7%) smaller, and Tasmania, Australian Capital Territory, and Northern Territory each at 1% to 2%. Top four combined: 88.2%. Comparing this to the emissions share helps explain why some states look modest per person despite large totals (e.g., New South Wales), and why small states can rank high per capita.

Facilities share: Reporting facilities are spread fairly evenly across New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, and Western Australia (each 21% to 22%), with South Australia (8%), Tasmania (3%), Northern Territory (2%), and Australian Capital Territory (<1%) much smaller. Top four combined: 85.5%. When facility share is similar but emissions share differs, it points to differences in activity, technology, or average emissions per facility.

Facilities and Emissions Relationship

More facilities do not always translate into proportionally higher emissions. Western Australia and Queensland host many reporting facilities, yet their average emissions per facility are lower than South Australia and Tasmania. This points to differences in industrial mix, technologies, or operating intensity.

South Australia and Tasmania stand out for higher average emissions per facility, which helps explain their stronger per‑capita figures despite smaller populations. Victoria shows the opposite pattern: many facilities but comparatively low average emissions per site, keeping its per‑capita rate down.

At the extremes, the Australian Capital Territory remains low on total emissions, facility count, and per‑facility averages, while New South Wales carries a large total with a mid‑range average per facility, reflecting scale more than intensity per site.

Sector composition also matters: states with more primary and secondary industry (for example, mining, metals, and heavy manufacturing) often show higher emissions intensity per site, while more service‑oriented economies tend to record lower averages. Fuel mix, process technology, and regulatory controls further shape where each state sits in this chart.
